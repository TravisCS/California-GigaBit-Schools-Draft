      About the data

      Map by Travis Cody Smith. Made .

      Independent map. Not affiliated with or endorsed by the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC), the National Telecommunications and Information Administration (NTIA) or any provider shown. It uses their public data; for official decisions, see the CPUC's BEAD pages.

      Loading data

      In short: California's BEAD plan, approved by NTIA on July 17, 2026 and ratified by the CPUC on September 17, gives about $1.415 billion to 20 internet providers for 83 projects that will connect 269,322 unserved and underserved homes and businesses, just over half of them with fiber, about a fifth with fixed wireless and about a quarter with low-earth-orbit satellite. Of the 853 public schools mapped here without gigabit at their block, the plan names or shares a site with 79; the other 774 are not in it, including 43 of the 46 in blocks under 25 Mbps. The map opens on one dot per project; the schools, the gaps and the speed shading are under More.

      Underneath: 781 of California's 10,351 public schools (460 districts in 56 counties, 283,360 students) sit in census blocks where the fastest fixed broadband anyone subscribes to is under 1 Gbps download. Another 72 schools (26,049 students) sit where the state map has no speed data. The other 9,498 schools are in gigabit blocks. The map's live count includes both groups, so it shows 853 schools in 494 districts across 57 counties. Speeds are the fastest plan with at least one subscriber in the block, as reported to the CPUC, not what providers advertise.

      Notes

      1. What the map shows. Shaded areas are census blocks colored by the fastest fixed (wired or fixed wireless) download speed reported for consumers. Dots are public K-12 schools in blocks below 1 Gbps or with no data, grouped by county, then shown one by one.
      2. Speed source. California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC), California Interactive Broadband Map, Fixed Consumer Downstream Maximum, end of year 2024. The CPUC describes the layer as "Maximum available download speeds in a Census Block, as determined by the presence of at least one subscriber. Source: CPUC collected deployment and subscription data as of December 31, 2024." The CPUC posted the matching data files on April 1, 2026. The layer covers 362,474 blocks.
      3. Download only. The tiers use download speed alone. BEAD defines unserved as below 25/3 Mbps and underserved as below 100/20 Mbps, so the two lowest tiers (46 schools) and the 25 to 99 Mbps tiers (133 schools) roughly match those groups, but upload speed and latency were not checked.
      4. Why CPUC and not the FCC. The FCC National Broadband Map has a newer release (availability as of December 31, 2025), but its downloads and area summaries could not be retrieved for this build. The CPUC layer is the state's official map and is built from provider subscription data, so it is a narrower measure than FCC availability: a block counts as gigabit only if someone there subscribes to a gigabit plan.
      5. How speeds were read. The CPUC layer was drawn at 100 m per pixel across the state from the CPUC map service, and each pixel was matched to the layer's own legend colors. A small number of blocks carry placeholder values of 1,000,000 Mbps or more (600 at 20,000,000 and 19 at 1,000,000); the CPUC legend leaves these unfilled, and this map treats them as gigabit.
      6. School speeds. Each school's tier is read at its own location (599 schools). When the school's pixel fell on a block edge with no color, the nearest colored pixel within 300 m was used (182 schools). Schools with no colored block within 300 m are shown as "No speed data" (72 schools).
      7. Display shading. The shaded area is generalized to 400 m cells (the most common tier in each cell) so the page loads quickly. School tiers use the finer 100 m reading, so a school can sit in a cell shaded a different color.
      8. School source. National Center for Education Statistics (NCES), EDGE Public School Characteristics 2024-25, published April 27, 2026. California has 10,351 schools in this file. School names, districts, addresses, enrollment, county and locale come from it. On October 28, 2026 every mapped school was re-checked against that layer by ID: all 853 positions agree to within 1 m, and names, districts, addresses and enrollment match exactly. The file includes 7 "Future" schools not yet open, 6 "New" schools and 2 with a changed boundary or agency; their cards say so under Status.
      9. How schools were picked. School locations were drawn from the NCES service and compared with the speed layer. Schools in or near any block below 1 Gbps (1,342 records) were then pulled from the NCES service by location, and each was given its tier as in note 6. Schools that turned out to be in gigabit blocks were dropped from the dots.
      10. Area cards. Tapping a shaded area outlines one connected patch of 400 m cells that share the same fastest download (8,824 patches statewide) and shows its size, the counties of the schools inside it, and the share of land within 5 km of the tapped point in each tier. Sizes are true ground area, corrected for the Web Mercator stretch. For gigabit patches it also shows the split between 2 Gbps or more, 1 to 1.99 Gbps and placeholder values. A school is listed in a patch when its 400 m cell (or a touching cell) falls in that patch, so a school can appear in a patch of a different color than its own tier (note 7).
      11. Getting around. The legend has two tabs. Projects shows the project dots with the provider filter, the county picker, the project list, the project areas and the funded homes. More holds the basemap switch, the equity overlays, the optional school layers (the schools in the BEAD plan and those left out) and the internet speed shading. Tap a project dot for its card and area, or a school dot for its card. The target button under the zoom control shows the whole state. Every card has a Close button. School cards have a button that shows the whole district with its own card, and district cards have one that zooms out to the county. Escape closes any card. Keyboard: Tab moves through the controls and then every dot (the dot's label appears as it gets focus), Enter or Space opens it, and the arrow keys pan the map.
      12. Grouping. Schools are grouped by county. Districts are NCES local education agencies, so a single-school charter counts as its own district. Each school sits under its own county, so a district that crosses a county line, such as Coachella Valley Unified (Riverside and Imperial) or Stony Creek Joint Unified (Glenn and Colusa), shows up under each county it reaches; its card covers the whole district. A county's number is the count of schools shown, and its shading is the most common tier among them. Unchecking a tier in the legend removes its schools and shading.
      13. BEAD plan. California's plan for the federal Broadband Equity, Access, and Deployment program, approved by NTIA on July 17, 2026 and ratified by the CPUC in Resolution T-17920 on September 17, 2026, lists 1,249 community anchor institutions (548 of them schools) that its funded projects will connect. Each mapped school was compared with that list: a school is "named" when a listed school within 1 km has the same distinctive name words and is the same kind of school (60 schools), and "shares a site" when it is not named but a listed anchor of any kind is within 150 m (19 schools). Cards show the project's provider, technology, planned speeds, deadline and total BEAD support. BEAD grants go to the providers that build the lines, not to schools, and the plan's award terms were still being settled when this map was made. The map opens showing only these 79 schools; the legend's "Schools not in the BEAD plan" box adds the other 774, including 43 of the 46 in blocks under 25 Mbps and 170 of the 179 under 100 Mbps. Each school was also checked against the 83 funded project areas in the CPUC's public BEAD map service (a school counts when the area covers its point or a point 1 km away). Of the 774 not in the plan, 147 have a funded fiber project within 1 km, 207 only fixed wireless, 251 only satellite (SpaceX or Amazon Kuiper), and 169 none. A project area covers the homes and businesses the project will connect, not every building inside it, so being inside one does not mean the school will be connected.
      14. Project names. The CPUC identifies projects only by codes such as CM61-BEAD-CA-67-14226 (the first number is the provider, the second its application). The readable names on cards and in the project list, such as "AT&T Fiber \u00b7 Descanso area, San Diego County", are made by this map: the counties each project's area covers (tools/project_names.py) and, for smaller projects, the town with the most funded locations (a count by city from the CPUC's funded-location layer). Projects spread over more than three counties are named by region, and the two satellite projects as rural California.
      15. Project dots. The map opens with one dot per funded BEAD project, placed at the point inside the project's largest piece that is farthest from its edges, sized by the homes and businesses it will connect and colored like the project areas (by technology or provider). Dots that would overlap are nudged apart a little. The school layers under More add the schools as small dots on top. "All providers" narrows the dots and areas to one provider, and "Go to a county" zooms to one county and outlines it.
      16. Address lookup. The address lookup under the title finds your address with Esri's World geocoder, then checks it against the funded project areas on this map (simplified to about 400 m, so approximate) and the CPUC list of every funded BEAD location within 150 m. Your address is sent to Esri to place it on the map; this map keeps nothing.
      17. Shareable links. The page address keeps the open project, provider, county and tab (for example #p=CM61-BEAD-CA-67-14226), and every project card has a Copy link button, so a link opens the map on the same view.
      18. Equity context. Low-income areas and tribal lands are optional map overlays drawn from the CPUC's BEAD Public Dashboard map services (PAU_LIA for low-income areas and PAU_TRIBAL for tribal lands). The shapes are dissolved and simplified to about 400 m, so edges are approximate and should not be used for parcel-level questions.
      19. Funded project areas. The legend's "Funded project areas" layer draws the 83 project areas from the CPUC's public BEAD map service, colored by technology: fiber (67 projects, including 8 hybrid), fixed wireless (14) and satellite (2, SpaceX and Amazon Kuiper, which cover much of rural California). Shapes were simplified to about 400 m and rounded to about 100 m, and pieces too small to keep their shape are drawn as small boxes, so edges are approximate. An area covers the homes and businesses its project will connect, not every building inside it. "Color by" switches between technology and provider (the seven largest providers have their own colors; the other 13 share one), and "All 83 projects, by provider" lists every project ID with a link to its area. Each card shows the provider, the number of locations, BEAD support in total and per location, the required low-cost plan price, the deadline, and how many mapped schools sit inside.
      20. Funded homes and businesses. The "Funded homes and businesses" layer shows each of the 269,322 locations the plan funds (unserved or underserved homes and businesses) as a dot colored by technology; the dots load live from the CPUC’s public BEAD map service for the area on screen, only at the two closest zoom levels and at most 6,000 at a time, so they need a connection and follow any changes the CPUC makes to that service.
      21. Limitations. Census blocks can be large in rural areas, and a school near a block edge may take its neighbor's speed. Reported speeds are advertised plan speeds, not measured speeds. A school's own connection (often a dedicated circuit through E-Rate or the CENIC K12 High Speed Network) can be much faster than what homes in the same block can buy, so this map shows neighborhood access, not the school's circuit. Enrollment of 0 or below means NCES did not report it.
      22. Basemaps and outline. USGS The National Map tile services, USGS Imagery Topo (satellite imagery with roads, boundaries and labels) and USGS Topo, loaded live and public domain as U.S. government works. A single statewide image of each ships with the page underneath the tiles, so the map still reads if the tiles cannot load. The California outline is the generalized state boundary, and the county shapes are the county boundaries simplified to about 400 m, both from the U.S. Census Bureau's TIGERweb service.
      23. Tools and AI use. Built with Leaflet 1.9.4 and Python (NumPy, SciPy, Pillow). Code and layout generated with Claude (Anthropic), which also gathered and processed the data from the sources listed below. Data, placement and accuracy have not yet been reviewed by Travis Cody Smith.

      Link audit

      October 29 recheck. After the BEAD layers were added, the 15 source, service and basemap links were checked again by three Claude Sonnet agents: 12 loaded and showed the right page, the FCC map again blocked scripts (it opens in a browser), and the two NCES links could not be reached from the checking computer because NCES had started refusing its connections. A script fetched all 1,347 school and district record links: 1,116 loaded and named the right school or district, the two known name differences below appeared again, Green Point Elementary's link now opened a blank NCES search form and was rerouted to the EDGE 2024-25 layer, and 228 could not be reached for the same NCES reason. Those 228 were good in the October 28 audit and have not changed. The new project cards link only to the CPUC BEAD final proposal page, which checked good.

      On October 28, 2026, every link on this map was opened and checked by nine Claude Sonnet agents running in parallel: eight took a slice each of the 1,347 school and district record links, and one took the 14 source, basemap and library links. A link counted as good only if the page loaded and showed the right school or district name. Rows that failed were refetched one at a time by hand before any change was made.
